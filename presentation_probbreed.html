Why cultivar recommendation is risky

  • Breeding programs test dozens of genotypes across many locations and years before recommending one to growers
  • Genotype-by-environment interaction (GEI) means a genotype’s rank can change from site to site
  • Classical tools (BLUEs/BLUPs, AMMI/GGE biplots) tell us which genotype ranks higher on average — mostly descriptive
  • They rarely answer what breeders actually need to tell growers: > “How likely is this genotype to still be a good choice next year, somewhere new?”

Open with the core motivating problem. [Click through the bullets one at a time, pause after each.] Start concrete: “Imagine you’ve run trials at 16 locations for 3 years. You have a ranking. But rankings hide something important – genotype-by-environment interaction, GEI, means the #1 genotype at one site might be #20 somewhere else.” Land on the quoted question – read it out loud, slowly, it’s the thesis of the whole talk. Emphasize that AMMI/GGE biplots are great for exploring GEI visually but don’t give you a number you can act on.

From ranking to risk

ProbBreed’s reframe

Instead of “which genotype has the highest mean?”, ask:

“What is the probability that this genotype belongs to the top performers — and how confident can we be in that recommendation?”

This shift — from a point estimate to a probability of success — is what makes the framework directly usable in a breeder’s decision routine.

ProbBreed implements the Bayesian method of Dias et al. (2022, TAG) as an R package, described in Chaves et al. (2024, G3).

[Point at the callout box – this is the single most important idea in the talk, say it slowly.] “This is the reframe: instead of a single best-guess ranking, we ask for a probability.” Explain why that matters practically: a probability is something a breeder can weigh against cost, time, and risk tolerance, the same way they already think about selection intensity. Then bridge to the citation: this isn’t a new statistical idea we invented for the talk – it’s a peer-reviewed method (Dias et al. 2022) that Saulo Chaves and colleagues packaged into easy-to-use R functions (Chaves et al. 2024), which is what we’re demonstrating today.

Why Bayesian?

Frequentist ranking Bayesian probability
Output Point estimate / rank Full posterior distribution
Uncertainty Awkward for ratios & rankings Propagated to any derived quantity
“Risk of recommendation” Not directly available Directly computable as a probability
Interpretation “Genotype A’s BLUP is higher” “Genotype A has an 87% chance of being top-tier”
  • Models are fit with Stan (rstan), using the self-tuning No-U-Turn Sampler
  • Priors are weakly informative and data-driven: \(\phi = \max(y) \times 10\)
  • Payoff: the entire posterior of every genotypic effect feeds directly into probability calculations

[Give the table a moment to be read before you talk over it.] Walk row by row: “Output” – frequentist gives you one number per genotype, Bayesian gives you a whole distribution of plausible values. “Uncertainty” – ratios and rankings are notoriously hard to get confidence intervals for in the frequentist world; here it falls out for free. “Risk of recommendation” is the row that matters most to breeders – literally not computable without a posterior. Finish on the bottom bullets: mention Stan/NUTS only briefly (no need to teach HMC here), and flag that the prior hyperparameter is data-driven, not something the user has to tune – we’ll come back to the exact priors in a few slides.

The core idea: defining “success”

Let \(\Omega\) be the set of high-performing genotypes, sized by the selection intensity the breeder chooses (e.g., top 20%):

\[ \Pr(\hat{g}_j \in \Omega \mid y) = \frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S} I\left(\hat{g}_j^{(s)} \in \Omega \mid y\right) \]

In words: simulate \(S\) virtual trials from the posterior, and count how often genotype \(j\) would have been selected. The same counting logic — applied within an environment, to a pairwise comparison, or to a GEI variance — produces every other probability in the package.

[Point at the formula on screen while you explain it symbol by symbol.] “Omega is just ‘the winners’ circle’ – whatever top slice of genotypes the breeder wants, say top 20%. The hat-g-j is genotype j’s estimated effect. This formula is really just counting.” [Then point at the diagram, left to right.] Walk through the five boxes as a mechanical recipe: fit the model once, draw thousands of samples from its posterior, check in each sample whether genotype j would have made the cut, count the successes, divide by the total. That count-and-divide pattern is the engine behind literally everything else we’ll see today – performance, stability, pairwise comparisons, all of it.

Four probability lenses

Every one of these is a direct, interpretable probability — the same currency breeders already use when they talk about “risk.” Stability is defined à la Shukla (1972): low genotype-by-environment variance.

[Draw attention to the diagram – give the audience a few seconds to read all four columns before narrating.] Frame this as a preview, not the full derivation: “These are the four questions ProbBreed answers, and later in the talk we’ll write out the actual formula for each one.” Walk left to right: raw performance, then a head-to-head comparison (useful for ‘is my new line better than the check?’), then stability in the classic Shukla sense – low GEI variance means the genotype behaves the same everywhere – and finally the joint probability, which most breeders care about most because it rewards genotypes that are both good and dependable.

Model priors

Priors follow Dias et al. (2022) — weakly informative, but never fully uninformative:

\[ x \sim N\left(0,\, S^{[x]}\right), \qquad \sigma \sim \text{HalfCauchy}\left(0,\, S^{[\sigma]}\right) \]

where \(x\) is any model effect except the residual, and \(\sigma\) is the residual standard deviation. For heterogeneous-variance models (res.het = TRUE):

\[ \sigma_k \sim \text{HalfCauchy}\left(0,\, S^{[\sigma_k]}\right) \]

The hyperprior on every scale parameter is itself data-driven:

\[ S^{[x]} \sim \text{HalfCauchy}(0,\, \phi), \qquad \phi = \max(y) \times 10 \]

This is the “what’s actually happening inside bayes_met()” slide – take it a bit slower since it’s the densest math of the talk. [Point at the first formula.] Every fixed and random effect gets a Normal(0, S) prior; every variance/SD term gets a Half-Cauchy, which is standard practice because it keeps variances non-negative while still being weakly informative. [Point at the second formula.] When residual variance is allowed to differ by location – res.het = TRUE – each location gets its own sigma_k with its own Half-Cauchy prior. [Point at the third formula, the most important one on this slide.] The key design choice is that the hyperparameter phi isn’t a fixed number the user has to guess – it scales with the data itself, ten times the maximum observed phenotype, which keeps the prior vague enough to let the data dominate while still bounding it away from being improper.

Probability of superior performance

Across environments — genotype \(j\)’s marginal effect:

\[ \Pr(\hat{g}_j \in \Omega \mid y) = \frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S} I\left(\hat{g}_j^{(s)} \in \Omega \mid y\right) \]

Pairwise — head-to-head against a specific competitor or check cultivar:

\[ \Pr(\hat{g}_j > \hat{g}_{j'} \mid y) = \frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S} I\left(\hat{g}_j^{(s)} > \hat{g}_{j'}^{(s)} \mid y\right) \]

(flip the inequality to \(<\) when selecting for a decreasing trait, e.g. plant height or disease severity)

[Point at the formula on the left – the first equation.] This is the formal version of the counting-game diagram from earlier: same S posterior draws, same indicator function, same division by S. [Point at the second formula.] The pairwise version answers a much more concrete breeding question – “is my elite candidate actually better than the commercial check?” – by comparing the two genotypes’ draws directly in every posterior sample, rather than each against the whole population. Flag the footnote about flipping the inequality; it’s a one-argument change in the R function (increase = FALSE) but easy to forget conceptually.

Probability of superior stability

A genotype is “stable” (Shukla, 1972) when its genotype-by-environment interaction variance is low:

\[ \Pr\left[\text{var}(\widehat{ge}_{jk}) \in \Omega \mid y\right] = \frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S} I\left[\text{var}\left(\widehat{ge}_{jk}^{(s)}\right) \in \Omega \mid y\right] \]

Pairwise stability — which of two genotypes is more consistent:

\[ \Pr\left[\text{var}(\widehat{ge}_{jk}) < \text{var}(\widehat{ge}_{j'k}) \mid y\right] = \frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S} I\left[\text{var}\left(\widehat{ge}_{jk}^{(s)}\right) < \text{var}\left(\widehat{ge}_{j'k}^{(s)}\right) \mid y\right] \]

[Point at the stability definition at the top before moving to the formula.] Ground this before the math: stability here is not about high performance, it’s about the genotype behaving the same way everywhere – low genotype-by-environment interaction variance. Same counting recipe as performance, just applied to the variance component instead of the main effect – [point at var(ge_jk) in the formula] note that “belonging to Omega” here means having a variance small enough to be in the low-variance group, not a high value. The pairwise version at the bottom directly compares two genotypes’ consistency, which is often what breeders actually want to know: “is line A more reliable than line B across our target region?”

Joint probability of performance and stability

Assuming genotypic main effects and GEI variances are independent by model design:

\[ \Pr\left[\hat{g}_j \in \Omega \,\cap\, \text{var}(\widehat{ge}_{jk}) \in \Omega\right] = \Pr(\hat{g}_j \in \Omega) \times \Pr\left[\text{var}(\widehat{ge}_{jk}) \in \Omega\right] \]

  • Simply the product of the two probabilities from the previous slide
  • Rewards genotypes that are simultaneously high-performing and dependable
  • Rarely will the same genotype top every individual metric — this is the one that reconciles them

[Point at the formula – it’s just a multiplication, make that clear right away.] “This looks intimidating but it’s literally just performance-probability times stability-probability.” Because the model treats the genotypic main effect and the GxE variance as independent random quantities, the joint probability of two independent events is just their product – no new machinery needed. [Click through the bullets one at a time.] This is usually the number breeders care about most in a final recommendation decision, precisely because it’s rare for the same genotype to win on every single metric – this is the tie-breaker.

Within-environment results

Same logic, applied locally instead of across the whole trial network — useful for site-specific recommendations:

\[ \Pr(\hat{g}_{jk} \in \Omega_k \mid y) = \frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S} I\left(\hat{g}_{jk}^{(s)} \in \Omega_k \mid y\right) \]

\[ \Pr(\hat{g}_{jk} > \hat{g}_{j'k} \mid y) = \frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S} I\left(\hat{g}_{jk}^{(s)} > \hat{g}_{j'k}^{(s)} \mid y\right) \]

Both are computed per environment — one heatmap per location or region, not a single global number.

[Point at the subscript k in the formulas – that’s the only difference from the “across” versions.] Everything on this slide is structurally identical to the across-environment formulas two slides ago, with one change: the genotypic effect now includes the environment-specific deviation (g-hat-jk instead of g-hat-j), and Omega-k is the top slice within that one environment. Explain why this matters practically: a breeder recommending cultivars to growers in one specific region doesn’t care about performance averaged across the whole trial network – they care about performance right there. In the tutorial, this is what produces the heatmaps rather than the lollipop charts.

Key takeaways

  1. Cultivar recommendation is a decision under uncertainty — GEI makes single-estimate rankings incomplete
  2. ProbBreed recasts that uncertainty as directly interpretable probabilities, from a Bayesian MET model’s posterior
  3. One consistent input — the selection intensity — drives performance, stability, pairwise, and joint probabilities
  4. Three functions: bayes_met() → extr_outs() → prob_sup()
