Why cultivar recommendation is risky

  • Breeding programs test dozens of genotypes across many locations and years before recommending one to growers
  • Genotype-by-environment interaction (GEI) means a genotype’s rank can change from site to site
  • Classical tools (BLUEs/BLUPs, AMMI/GGE biplots) tell us which genotype ranks higher on average — mostly descriptive
  • They rarely answer what breeders actually need to tell growers: > “How likely is this genotype to still be a good choice next year, somewhere new?”

Open with the core motivating problem. [Click through the bullets one at a time, pause after each.] Start concrete: “Imagine you’ve run trials at 16 locations for 3 years. You have a ranking. But rankings hide something important – genotype-by-environment interaction, GEI, means the #1 genotype at one site might be #20 somewhere else.” Land on the quoted question – read it out loud, slowly, it’s the thesis of the whole talk. Emphasize that AMMI/GGE biplots are great for exploring GEI visually but don’t give you a number you can act on.

From ranking to risk

ProbBreed’s reframe

Instead of “which genotype has the highest mean?”, ask:

“What is the probability that this genotype belongs to the top performers — and how confident can we be in that recommendation?”

This shift — from a point estimate to a probability of success — is what makes the framework directly usable in a breeder’s decision routine.

ProbBreed implements the Bayesian method of Dias et al. (2022, TAG) as an R package, described in Chaves et al. (2024, G3).

[Point at the callout box – this is the single most important idea in the talk, say it slowly.] “This is the reframe: instead of a single best-guess ranking, we ask for a probability.” Explain why that matters practically: a probability is something a breeder can weigh against cost, time, and risk tolerance, the same way they already think about selection intensity. Then bridge to the citation: this isn’t a new statistical idea we invented for the talk – it’s a peer-reviewed method (Dias et al. 2022) that Saulo Chaves and colleagues packaged into easy-to-use R functions (Chaves et al. 2024), which is what we’re demonstrating today.

Why Bayesian?

Frequentist ranking Bayesian probability
Output Point estimate / rank Full posterior distribution
Uncertainty Awkward for ratios & rankings Propagated to any derived quantity
“Risk of recommendation” Not directly available Directly computable as a probability
Interpretation “Genotype A’s BLUP is higher” “Genotype A has an 87% chance of being top-tier”
  • Models are fit with Stan (rstan), using the self-tuning No-U-Turn Sampler
  • Priors are weakly informative and data-driven: \(\phi = \max(y) \times 10\)
  • Payoff: the entire posterior of every genotypic effect feeds directly into probability calculations

[Give the table a moment to be read before you talk over it.] Walk row by row: “Output” – frequentist gives you one number per genotype, Bayesian gives you a whole distribution of plausible values. “Uncertainty” – ratios and rankings are notoriously hard to get confidence intervals for in the frequentist world; here it falls out for free. “Risk of recommendation” is the row that matters most to breeders – literally not computable without a posterior. Finish on the bottom bullets: mention Stan/NUTS only briefly (no need to teach HMC here), and flag that the prior hyperparameter is data-driven, not something the user has to tune.

The core idea: defining “success”

Let \(\Omega\) be the set of high-performing genotypes, sized by the selection intensity the breeder chooses (e.g., top 20%):

\[ \Pr(\hat{g}_j \in \Omega \mid y) = \frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S} I\left(\hat{g}_j^{(s)} \in \Omega \mid y\right) \]

In words: simulate \(S\) virtual trials from the posterior, and count how often genotype \(j\) would have been selected. The same counting logic — applied within an environment, to a pairwise comparison, or to a GEI variance — produces every other probability in the package.

[Point at the formula on screen while you explain it symbol by symbol.] “Omega is just ‘the winners’ circle’ – whatever top slice of genotypes the breeder wants, say top 20%. The hat-g-j is genotype j’s estimated effect. This formula is really just counting.” [Then point at the diagram, left to right.] Walk through the five boxes as a mechanical recipe: fit the model once, draw thousands of samples from its posterior, check in each sample whether genotype j would have made the cut, count the successes, divide by the total. That count-and-divide pattern is the engine behind literally everything else we’ll see today – performance, stability, pairwise comparisons, all of it.

Four probability lenses

Every one of these is a direct, interpretable probability — the same currency breeders already use when they talk about “risk.” Stability is defined à la Shukla (1972): low genotype-by-environment variance.

[Draw attention to the diagram – give the audience a few seconds to read all four columns before narrating.] Frame this as a preview, not the full derivation: “These are the four questions ProbBreed answers, and later in the talk we’ll write out the actual formula for each one.” Walk left to right: raw performance, then a head-to-head comparison (useful for ‘is my new line better than the check?’), then stability in the classic Shukla sense – low GEI variance means the genotype behaves the same everywhere – and finally the joint probability, which most breeders care about most because it rewards genotypes that are both good and dependable.

bayes_met(): the function and its parameters

mod = bayes_met(
  data = MyData, gen = "Genotype", loc = "Location",
  repl = ..., reg = ..., year = ...,
  res.het = TRUE, trait = "Phenotype",
  iter = 2000, cores = 2, chains = 4)
  • gen, loc, trait — genotype, location, response columns
  • repl, reg, year — pick one of the nine models (next slide)
  • res.het — homogeneous vs. heterogeneous residual variance
  • iter, cores, chains — MCMC effort
  • ... passes through to rstan::sampling() (seed, adapt_delta)

[Point at the code block and read the argument list top to bottom.] This single function call is the entire modeling step – there’s no Stan code to write by hand. extr_outs(): checking the model fit

outs = extr_outs(
  model = mod,
  probs = c(0.05, 0.95), verbose = TRUE)

Returns an object of class extr — a list with:

  • The posterior distribution of every model effect
  • Data simulated from the fitted model, for posterior predictive checks
  • The maximum a posteriori (MAP) value of each effect
  • Variance components, with highest posterior density (HPD) intervals
  • Goodness-of-fit diagnostics: Bayesian p-values, \(\hat{R}\), WAIC2, effective sample size

plot(outs) compares the empirical vs. simulated phenotype distributions — the closer the overlap, the more the model captured the real data.

Frame this as the “check your work” step before trusting any probability: we never go straight from bayes_met() to conclusions. [Click through the bullets one at a time.] The posterior distributions are the raw material for step three; the simulated data and diagnostics are what let us sanity-check the fit. [Point at the closing sentence about plot(outs).] In the tutorial, this is literally one line of code, and we’ll look at exactly this empirical-vs-simulated plot together to decide whether we trust the model enough to move on.

prob_sup(): the probsup object

results = prob_sup(
  extr = outs,
  int = 0.2, increase = TRUE)

Returns an object of class probsup — two lists:

  • results$across — superior performance, pairwise performance, superior stability, pairwise stability, and joint probability, computed across the whole trial network
  • results$within — performance and pairwise performance, computed separately within each individual environment
  • Only one extra input is needed beyond the fitted model: the selection intensity (int)

This slide sets up the structure for everything that follows – [point at the diagram] two lists hanging off one object, across and within. Mirror this explicitly to the package documentation: “this is exactly the probsup object you’ll see in the site docs and in the tutorial.” [Point at the two accessor bullets.] Emphasize that the next few slides are literally walking through the elements of results\(across one at a time, then results\)within – so this diagram is the map for the rest of this section. Also stress the int argument: it’s the only new piece of information the breeder has to supply, the selection intensity, e.g. top 20%.

results$across: probability of superior performance

Genotype \(j\)’s marginal effect (results$across$perfo):

\[ \Pr(\hat{g}_j \in \Omega \mid y) = \frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S} I\left(\hat{g}_j^{(s)} \in \Omega \mid y\right) \]

Pairwise — head-to-head against a specific competitor or check cultivar (results$across$pair_perfo):

\[ \Pr(\hat{g}_j > \hat{g}_{j'} \mid y) = \frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S} I\left(\hat{g}_j^{(s)} > \hat{g}_{j'}^{(s)} \mid y\right) \]

(flip the inequality to \(<\) when selecting for a decreasing trait, e.g. plant height or disease severity)

[Point at the formula on the left – the first equation.] This is the formal version of the counting-game diagram from earlier: same S posterior draws, same indicator function, same division by S – and it’s what lands in results\(across\)perfo. [Point at the second formula.] The pairwise version answers a much more concrete breeding question – “is my elite candidate actually better than the commercial check?” – and lands in results\(across\)pair_perfo. Flag the footnote about flipping the inequality; it’s a one-argument change in the R function (increase = FALSE) but easy to forget conceptually.

results$across: probability of superior stability

A genotype is “stable” (Shukla, 1972) when its genotype-by-environment interaction variance is low (results$across$stabi):

\[ \Pr\left[\text{var}(\widehat{ge}_{jk}) \in \Omega \mid y\right] = \frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S} I\left[\text{var}\left(\widehat{ge}_{jk}^{(s)}\right) \in \Omega \mid y\right] \]

Pairwise stability — which of two genotypes is more consistent (results$across$pair_stabi):

\[ \Pr\left[\text{var}(\widehat{ge}_{jk}) < \text{var}(\widehat{ge}_{j'k}) \mid y\right] = \frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S} I\left[\text{var}\left(\widehat{ge}_{jk}^{(s)}\right) < \text{var}\left(\widehat{ge}_{j'k}^{(s)}\right) \mid y\right] \]

[Point at the stability definition at the top before moving to the formula.] Ground this before the math: stability here is not about high performance, it’s about the genotype behaving the same way everywhere – low genotype-by-environment interaction variance. Same counting recipe as performance, just applied to the variance component instead of the main effect, landing in results\(across\)stabi – [point at var(ge_jk) in the formula] note that “belonging to Omega” here means having a variance small enough to be in the low-variance group, not a high value. The pairwise version at the bottom (results\(across\)pair_stabi) directly compares two genotypes’ consistency.

results$across: joint probability

Assuming genotypic main effects and GEI variances are independent by model design (results$across$joint):

\[ \Pr\left[\hat{g}_j \in \Omega \,\cap\, \text{var}(\widehat{ge}_{jk}) \in \Omega\right] = \Pr(\hat{g}_j \in \Omega) \times \Pr\left[\text{var}(\widehat{ge}_{jk}) \in \Omega\right] \]

  • Simply the product of the two probabilities from the previous slide
  • Rewards genotypes that are simultaneously high-performing and dependable
  • Rarely will the same genotype top every individual metric — this is the one that reconciles them

[Point at the formula – it’s just a multiplication, make that clear right away.] “This looks intimidating but it’s literally just performance-probability times stability-probability.” Because the model treats the genotypic main effect and the GxE variance as independent random quantities, the joint probability of two independent events is just their product – no new machinery needed, and it’s the last element of results$across. [Click through the bullets one at a time.] This is usually the number breeders care about most in a final recommendation decision, precisely because it’s rare for the same genotype to win on every single metric – this is the tie-breaker.

results$within: performance in each environment

Same logic as results$across$perfo, applied locally instead of across the whole trial network — useful for site-specific recommendations (results$within$perfo):

\[ \Pr(\hat{g}_{jk} \in \Omega_k \mid y) = \frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S} I\left(\hat{g}_{jk}^{(s)} \in \Omega_k \mid y\right) \]

Pairwise, within one environment (results$within$pair_perfo):

\[ \Pr(\hat{g}_{jk} > \hat{g}_{j'k} \mid y) = \frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S} I\left(\hat{g}_{jk}^{(s)} > \hat{g}_{j'k}^{(s)} \mid y\right) \]

Both are computed per environment — one heatmap per location or region, not a single global number.

[Point at the subscript k in the formulas – that’s the only difference from the across-environment performance formulas we saw earlier.] The genotypic effect now includes the environment-specific deviation (g-hat-jk instead of g-hat-j), and Omega-k is the top slice within that one environment – this is the results$within list, the second half of the probsup object from a few slides ago. Explain why this matters practically: a breeder recommending cultivars to growers in one specific region doesn’t care about performance averaged across the whole trial network – they care about performance right there. In the tutorial, this is what produces the heatmaps rather than the lollipop charts.

Key takeaways

  1. Cultivar recommendation is a decision under uncertainty — GEI makes single-estimate rankings incomplete
  2. ProbBreed recasts that uncertainty as directly interpretable probabilities, from a Bayesian MET model’s posterior
  3. One consistent input — the selection intensity — drives performance, stability, pairwise, and joint probabilities
  4. Three functions: bayes_met() → extr_outs() → prob_sup()
